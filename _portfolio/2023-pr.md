One of my machine learning projects was to work with software, behavioral science, and product teams to improve the intelligence underling an educational technology tool called <a href="https://problemroulette.ai.umich.edu/Welcome/" target="_blank">Problem Roulette</a> - an online quiz delivery system built and hosted by the Center for Academic Innovation. The end goals of the project were to serve students personalized study tips and to enable adaptive question delivery.

The first step to serving personlized study tips was to decide what information to provide study tips for. Decision Tree modeling was used for this decision. Over a dozen behaviors in Problem Roulette (page views, number of questions answered, accuracy, etc.) were compared to determine which were most strongly related to course grade. The top three are listed below. 

    Study Volume (total number of questions completed)
    Accuracy (total number correct)
    Question Difficulty (fraction of correct responses to total attempts)

With a list of key behaviors in hand, the next step was to understand their inter-relationships so that personalized messaging can be developed. Figure 1 was helpful in this regard. It shows the conditions under which a particular behavior was more or less effective at impacting course grade. For example, it can be seen that adding study volume is very beneficial when accuracy is high, but is considerably less beneficial when accuracy is low. Accordingly, we would not want to nudge this student to study more (volume isn't helping) but rather to study more intentionally (accuracy will help).

The brainstorming around what this intelligence would empower revolved primarily around personalization. For example, the behavioral science team wanted to be able to send targeted messages to specific students or in response to specific behaviors. Another goal was to deliver only the most personally-relevant questions and to deliver the fewest number of questions necessary to maximize personal grade gain. 

In other words, the goal was to personalize question difficulty and study volume according to each students abilities and needs.

1) Personalized nudges for study volume: Enabled via proficiency score

Starting Question: 
Can we predict course grades from behaviors in Problem Roulette?

Volume (are you practicing enough?)
Accuracy (how well are you practicing?)
Variation (is there enough variation in your question set that performance will generalize to novel questions?)


Goal is to explain the most variance in GPE with the fewest dimensions (because too many dimensions will make to model less interpretable and therefore more difficult to tailor on)


With these basic dimensions, the basic idea is that proficiency score amounts to doing well enough (accuracy) on enough (study volume) of the right questions (variation).

In addition to improving user experience through personalization, we also aimed to improve it through gamification. 3) Adding gamification features

When Problem Roulette was developed, its main value proposition was its test bank, which consisted of previously used test questions written by the professor of the course a student was taking. As we learned more about its ability to facilitate student success in the classroom, we wanted to find ways of encouraging its use. Personalization was one way we did so. Another way was through gamification. 

During the discovery process for building the proficiency score model, it was clear that "streaks" of correctly answered questions were a strong predictor of course grades. One interpretation is that streaks represent very intentional, effortful studying. It's tough to string together several correct responses. Doing so is rarely due to chance, so whatever a student did to earn a streak really worked well for them. Our goal was to signal this back to students in order to encourage reflection
